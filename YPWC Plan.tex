\section{Overview}
Youth Physics Writing Contest is a school-level competition where one physics
idea becomes a piece of writing people enjoy reading. Students from grades 9 to
12 take a single scientific corner and shape it into a clear, warm,
magazine-style article, the way a feature writer would, rather than the way a
lab report gets written. Curiosity matters more than prior experience or
grades. There is one round, every article is judged once against the same
scale, and the strongest pieces end up in a Physics Club Magazine issue.

\section{Format}
Every article follows the shape of a good magazine feature. It opens with a
title that promises something specific, and an introduction that pulls the
reader in quickly. The body explains the physics in plain language, written for
a curious nonspecialist, and points to where the idea shows up in real life. It
closes with an interesting insight and a short, memorable ending.

Length sits between \textbf{600 and 1500 words}. Warm, clear language beats
jargon, so analogies are welcome and graphs, figures, and GIFs help. A strong
article usually holds one central idea instead of three small ones, and numbers
are used honestly and checked twice. AI-generated text is not accepted; the
writing has to be the student's own.

\section{Topics}
Writers begin with a scientific desk and then find the human story inside it.
A student might ask why a phone flashlight looks blue in the dark, how one wave
can silence another, or what a black hole really is. The six desks below accept
that kind of question: the night sky, from the phases of the Moon to the edges
of the galaxy; the hidden mechanics of electricity and magnetism, from a fridge
magnet to a power grid; sound and waves, from ripples in a glass to
noise-cancelling headphones; a honest, careful simplification of modern and
quantum physics; thermodynamics, from a cooling cup of tea to a large engine;
and the physics quietly hiding in daily life.

\section{Timeline}
Submissions arrive through the contest page as a Word or PDF file, alongside an
optional GIF. The form records the student's name, school, chosen topic, and
the exact submission time, and no edits are accepted once registration closes.

\section{Evaluation}
Writers already know the scale before judging begins. Every article earns up to
\textbf{one hundred points}, decided across six categories: scientific accuracy
and conceptual understanding (30), physical reasoning and analysis (20),
clarity, structure and communication (20), creativity and intellectual
contribution (15), real-world connection (10), and research and references (5).
Mentors and editors score each piece independently against the same scale, and
participants receive brief editorial feedback as part of the experience.

\begin{quote}
\itshape Writing ``$F = ma$'' on its own shows little. Explaining the law,
applying it correctly, and noting its assumptions and limits shows real
understanding.
\end{quote}

\section{Results and Awards}
Medals go to the top 30\% of participants in a Gold, Silver, and Bronze ratio
of 3 to 2 to 1. The three strongest articles on the leaderboard receive special
prizes, and everyone who enters receives a certificate that names the article
and its writer. Results are announced on \textbf{October 10}, and the
leaderboard appears on the contest page the same day.

\section{Publication}
Selected articles are gathered into a dedicated Physics Club Magazine issue for
the wider school community to read, with each piece credited to its writer. The
finished magazine stays available online so writers can share it, because the
published issue is the real output of the competition, not just a ranking.
